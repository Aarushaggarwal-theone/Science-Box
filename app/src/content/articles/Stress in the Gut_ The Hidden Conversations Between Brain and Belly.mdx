Have you ever felt nauseous on the morning of an exam? Or experienced a fluttering sensation in your stomach before giving an important speech in front of a large audience? Well, I certainly have. These common sensations are not our minds playing tricks on us. Instead, they are the result of our brain and gut communicating with each other through a powerful biological connection, scientifically termed as the 'gut-brain axis'.

The gut-brain axis consists of a network of nerves through which numerous signals are sent back and forth, creating a link between your brain and gut. Your digestive system relies on the enteric nervous system, which operates within your gastrointestinal (GI) tract and regulates the digestive processes in your body. It is connected to your brain through the vagus nerve, which runs from your brainstem all the way down to your abdomen. The vagus nerve acts like electrical wiring as it carries electrical impulses in both directions: from the gut to the brain and from the brain back to the gut. Additionally, chemical messengers like neurotransmitters and hormones also play a key role in maintaining communication between the brain and the gut. This is because the bacteria in your gut, known as microbes, help produce various neurotransmitters, which help convey messages to your brain.

Now, let's take a look at what happens when we feel stressed or anxious. The moment you feel stressed, the body triggers the flight or fight response which is a survival mechanism controlled by the brain. During this fight or flight response, your body does its best to optimize your chances of survival. One of the ways it does this is by reducing circulation to less critical areas like the digestive tract in order to prioritize blood flow to more vital organs like your heart. In this scenario, the vagus nerve acts as a messenger telling the gut to slow down or even halt digestion so that blood can be redirected to other areas of the body. As a result, the intestines draw extra water out of the stool, making it harder and drier, leading to constipation. Thus, in situations of milder stress, like giving a public speech, digestion can slow down or become briefly disturbed, often resulting in abdominal discomfort. On the other hand, stress may even cause tension in the muscles of the digestive tract, leading to cramping, bloating, and in some cases an urgent need to use the bathroom.

Furthermore, research has also shown that gut bacteria can influence one's mood and stress levels. As mentioned earlier, the microbes in your gut produce neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine, which help regulate our emotions. However, if someone is under constant stress, the diversity of their microbiome is reduced. This means that the number of good bacteria that help produce useful neurotransmitters decreases. This, in turn, weakens the body's ability to produce mood-regulating chemicals, leaving the brain more vulnerable to anxiety and low mood. In this way, stress doesn’t just affect the stomach in the moment, but it can create a vicious cycle where poor gut health feeds back into mental health. So the next time your stomach reacts before a test or big event, remember it’s not just nerves, it’s your gut and brain talking. Taking care of your gut means taking care of your mind too.
